- The patent has a single claim.

### Plain facts for narration
- It is a rectangular watertight box. The inventor's own was "three feet long, two feet wide, and about ten inches in height." It has handles and casters.
- Posts hinged inside the tub fold up to carry a frame. A canvas, oil-cloth, or rubber cover goes over the frame and fastens around the top edge of the tub.
- The bather sits on a hinged slatted seat on crossed legs. Two small force-pumps sit in a well in the floor, and the bather works them with a rocking pedal "by the feet of the occupant."
- The pumps push water up a flexible hose to a hand-held, adjustable nozzle. The water then falls back into the well and gets pumped again, so "only a small amount of water will be necessary."
- The listed advantages are portability, the small space it needs, sitting instead of lying down, and "the seclusion afforded by the apparatus."
- When the bath is over, the frame, seat, legs, and posts all fold into the tub and the lid closes (Fig. 3).
- The patent itself admits that folding bathing-houses, pumps in portable baths, and folding seats were already known. It claims only the specific frame combination.

- Mild: the text says the occupant can apply the water "to any and all parts of his person" and "to any part of his person". This is ordinary hygiene, but I left it out of the quotes to avoid an unintended double meaning. Do not use it.
- Note that the patent was assigned one-half to another person (text only). Do not name either party.

### Quotes (verbatim, checked Google+OCR)

### Plain facts for narration

- A motor-driven blower pushes air through an electric air heater. One push button inside the stall starts it, and you press it again when you are dry (col. 2).
- With every damper closed except the top one, it becomes a hair dryer for "the region of the person's head" (col. 2).
- Suggested uses: home, hotel or club. The stall goes in the bathroom, and the blower-heater unit can go "in a more remote location" (col. 2).
- Filed in 1962 and granted in 1964. One claim.

- No outlet is needed. The water refilling the cistern drives a small turbine connected to a low-voltage DC generator, and that powers the electronics and motors (Abstract; col. 2; Stages III–V).
- It starts only when the flush lever is pressed by hand (Abstract; col. 2).
- Two electric motors turn the seat through gear wheels that engage a circular rack molded into the underside of the seat. The seat rides on steel balls (col. 7; col. 3).
- Seat switches tell the electronics whether the seat is occupied or raised. If the seat is occupied, rotation is prevented (col. 2; col. 7, FIG. 2). After cleaning, an oval seat returns to its "zero position" (col. 3).
- A variant replaces the disinfectant spray with a sponge under "rough, snythetic cloth" that wraps around the seat (col. 9, FIG. 6/8). Another dries the seat with a current of (hot) air (col. 3).
- The full cleaning cycle is drawn as eleven operating stages, I to XI (FIG. 5, cols. 7–8).
- It is meant for new toilets and for retrofitting existing ones (Abstract).
